## 76. Creep and Temperature Effects / Повзучість і вплив температури

### EN — Short
Creep and Temperature Effects explains how a material or structural element responds to a specified mechanical condition. It helps engineers predict behavior and avoid unreliable machine designs.

### EN — Detailed
- **Origin:** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century materials studies established time-dependent creep behavior.
- **Core idea:** Creep is deformation under sustained stress over time, often pronounced at elevated temperature. Primary, secondary and tertiary creep stages describe how deformation rate evolves. A long service time may dominate even with modest stress.
- **Key components:** Identify applied load in N, dimensions in m, material properties in Pa or GPa, and the relevant cross-sectional geometry. Track stress in Pa or MPa, strain without units, and displacements in m or mm. Creep is deformation under sustained stress over time, often pronounced at elevated temperature. Define the positive sign convention and check unit conversions before interpreting the result.
- **How to apply it:** Use material-specific creep curves at the real temperature, stress and lifetime. Start with a sketch showing restraints, load direction and dimensions. Select a representative section or specimen, calculate the ideal response, and compare it against the design requirement. Document assumptions, then check a second load case or a more detailed model where geometry is complex. For a prototype, measure the predicted response and compare it with calculations before raising the applied load.
- **Example:** A hot rod length 0.5 m accumulates creep strain 0.002 during a service interval: ΔL = εL = 0.002 × 0.5 = 0.001 m = 1 mm. At 0.003 strain it would extend 1.5 mm. The numerical result applies to the stated geometry and assumptions; confirm that all converted areas and lengths use consistent SI units.
- **Limitations:** A room-temperature elastic modulus cannot predict creep; thermal gradients add stress. Real components may also contain manufacturing defects, residual stress, temperature gradients and changing service loads. Use relevant standards and a suitable design margin; inspect parts that could fail dangerously.
- **Related theories:** Hooke's Law and Young's Modulus (#62); Elastic and Plastic Deformation (#64); Thermal Expansion (#80)
