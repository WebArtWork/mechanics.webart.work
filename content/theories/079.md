## 79. Steel and Heat Treatment Basics / Сталь та основи термічної обробки

### EN — Short
Steel and Heat Treatment Basics explains how a material or structural element responds to a specified mechanical condition. It helps engineers predict behavior and avoid unreliable machine designs.

### EN — Detailed
- **Origin:** Steel heat treatment evolved over centuries; phase transformations were clarified by late nineteenth-century metallurgy.
- **Core idea:** Heating and controlled cooling change microstructure, hardness, strength and toughness. Quenching may raise hardness but reduce toughness; tempering adjusts that balance. Results depend on composition and section thickness.
- **Key components:** Identify applied load in N, dimensions in m, material properties in Pa or GPa, and the relevant cross-sectional geometry. Track stress in Pa or MPa, strain without units, and displacements in m or mm. Heating and controlled cooling change microstructure, hardness, strength and toughness. Define the positive sign convention and check unit conversions before interpreting the result.
- **How to apply it:** Specify steel grade, treatment schedule and verification hardness or tensile tests. Start with a sketch showing restraints, load direction and dimensions. Select a representative section or specimen, calculate the ideal response, and compare it against the design requirement. Document assumptions, then check a second load case or a more detailed model where geometry is complex. For a prototype, measure the predicted response and compare it with calculations before raising the applied load.
- **Example:** A steel shaft diameter 20 mm has area A = π × 0.02²/4 = 3.14 × 10⁻⁴ m². If allowable axial stress after a qualified treatment is 300 MPa, ideal allowable force F = σA = 300 × 10⁶ × 3.14 × 10⁻⁴ = 94.2 kN. The numerical result applies to the stated geometry and assumptions; confirm that all converted areas and lengths use consistent SI units.
- **Limitations:** Heat treatment may distort parts and cause cracking; use qualified procedures and protective equipment. Real components may also contain manufacturing defects, residual stress, temperature gradients and changing service loads. Use relevant standards and a suitable design margin; inspect parts that could fail dangerously.
- **Related theories:** The Stress–Strain Curve (#63); Hardness and Toughness (#77); Material Classes: Metals, Polymers, Ceramics and Composites (#78)
