## 70. Second Moment of Area / Другий момент площі

### EN — Short
Second Moment of Area explains how a material or structural element responds to a specified mechanical condition. It helps engineers predict behavior and avoid unreliable machine designs.

### EN — Detailed
- **Origin:** The geometric concept entered classical beam theory through Euler–Bernoulli developments.
- **Core idea:** Second moment I = ∫y² dA measures distribution of area about an axis; rectangle I = bh³/12. Moving material away from the neutral axis increases bending stiffness without necessarily increasing area. Use consistent fourth-power length units.
- **Key components:** Identify applied load in N, dimensions in m, material properties in Pa or GPa, and the relevant cross-sectional geometry. Track stress in Pa or MPa, strain without units, and displacements in m or mm. Second moment I = ∫y² dA measures distribution of area about an axis; rectangle I = bh³/12. Define the positive sign convention and check unit conversions before interpreting the result.
- **How to apply it:** Calculate about the actual neutral axis, using parallel-axis theorem for composites. Start with a sketch showing restraints, load direction and dimensions. Select a representative section or specimen, calculate the ideal response, and compare it against the design requirement. Document assumptions, then check a second load case or a more detailed model where geometry is complex. For a prototype, measure the predicted response and compare it with calculations before raising the applied load.
- **Example:** For a rectangular beam b = 40 mm and h = 100 mm: I = 0.04 × (0.1)³ / 12 = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴. Rotating it swaps dimensions: I = 0.1 × (0.04)³ / 12 = 5.33 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴. The numerical result applies to the stated geometry and assumptions; confirm that all converted areas and lengths use consistent SI units.
- **Limitations:** This geometric quantity is not mass moment of inertia; orientation matters greatly. Real components may also contain manufacturing defects, residual stress, temperature gradients and changing service loads. Use relevant standards and a suitable design margin; inspect parts that could fail dangerously.
- **Related theories:** Centroids of Areas (#14); Bending of Beams (#68); Beam Deflection (#71)
