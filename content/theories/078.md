## 78. Material Classes: Metals, Polymers, Ceramics and Composites / Класи матеріалів: метали, полімери, кераміка та композити

### EN — Short
Material Classes: Metals, Polymers, Ceramics and Composites explains how a material or structural element responds to a specified mechanical condition. It helps engineers predict behavior and avoid unreliable machine designs.

### EN — Detailed
- **Origin:** Modern classification developed with materials science in the twentieth century.
- **Core idea:** Material families differ in stiffness, density, toughness, temperature limits and manufacturing routes. Metals are often ductile and conductive; ceramics resist heat but may fracture abruptly. Fiber composites require attention to load direction.
- **Key components:** Identify applied load in N, dimensions in m, material properties in Pa or GPa, and the relevant cross-sectional geometry. Track stress in Pa or MPa, strain without units, and displacements in m or mm. Material families differ in stiffness, density, toughness, temperature limits and manufacturing routes. Define the positive sign convention and check unit conversions before interpreting the result.
- **How to apply it:** Compare required stiffness, load, environment, price and fabrication method. Start with a sketch showing restraints, load direction and dimensions. Select a representative section or specimen, calculate the ideal response, and compare it against the design requirement. Document assumptions, then check a second load case or a more detailed model where geometry is complex. For a prototype, measure the predicted response and compare it with calculations before raising the applied load.
- **Example:** A 0.001 m³ part made of steel at 7,850 kg/m³ weighs m = 7.85 kg; the same volume of aluminum at 2,700 kg/m³ weighs 2.70 kg, saving 5.15 kg before redesign. The numerical result applies to the stated geometry and assumptions; confirm that all converted areas and lengths use consistent SI units.
- **Limitations:** Density alone does not determine strength; composites are directional and polymers creep. Real components may also contain manufacturing defects, residual stress, temperature gradients and changing service loads. Use relevant standards and a suitable design margin; inspect parts that could fail dangerously.
- **Related theories:** The Stress–Strain Curve (#63); Creep and Temperature Effects (#76); Hardness and Toughness (#77)
